# Ethical AI Principles

These principles define the baseline expectations for AI systems and governance practices associated with the URAI ecosystem.

## 1. Human dignity and agency

AI systems should support human autonomy, informed choice, and meaningful control. People should not be coerced, manipulated, or deprived of reasonable alternatives by system design.

## 2. Public benefit

Projects should be evaluated for their expected social value, foreseeable harms, and distribution of benefits and burdens. High-impact systems require explicit justification and independent review.

## 3. Transparency and explainability

Material system capabilities, limits, data practices, and known risks should be documented in language appropriate for affected users, reviewers, and the public.

## 4. Privacy and consent

Data collection and use should be minimized, purpose-bound, and supported by clear consent or another legitimate basis. Sensitive data requires heightened safeguards and review.

## 5. Fairness and non-discrimination

Systems should be assessed for disparate impact, representational harm, and exclusion. When risks are identified, mitigation plans should be documented and tracked.

## 6. Safety and robustness

Systems should be designed, tested, and monitored for misuse, reliability failures, and harmful outputs. Safety controls should be proportionate to system capability and deployment context.

## 7. Accountability

Every deployed system should have identifiable owners, review records, escalation paths, and mechanisms for redress when people are harmed or reasonably concerned.

## 8. Contestability

Affected people should have practical ways to question significant automated outputs, request human review where appropriate, and report suspected errors or harms.

## 9. Environmental responsibility

Projects should consider resource consumption, operational efficiency, and the environmental cost of model development and deployment.

## 10. Continuous review

Ethical approval is not a one-time event. Standards, risks, and mitigations should be revisited as systems, users, laws, and social contexts change.
