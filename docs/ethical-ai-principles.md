# Ethical AI Principles

Status: formation-draft
Registry ID: `URAI-ETH-001`

These principles define the formation-stage baseline for AI systems and governance practices associated with the URAI ecosystem. They are guidance, not certification or proof that a product satisfies them.

## 1. Human dignity, autonomy, and meaningful choice

AI should support people without coercion, manipulation, shame, surveillance pressure, or false urgency. Users should retain meaningful alternatives, interruption, correction, exit, and human-contact options where appropriate.

## 2. Informed consent and purpose limitation

Collection, inference, generation, sharing, and autonomous action should be understandable and tied to a stated purpose. Sensitive processing requires clear permission or another documented legitimate basis, with withdrawal and consequences explained.

Consent should not be bundled, hidden, permanent by default, or obtained through dark patterns.

## 3. Data minimization and private-by-default design

Systems should collect and retain only what is necessary for the user-authorized purpose. Private memories, relationships, health-adjacent signals, location, identity, grief, legacy, voice, images, biometrics, and spatial maps require heightened controls.

## 4. Memory provenance and epistemic humility

Systems must distinguish:

- user-provided facts;
- imported records;
- generated reconstruction;
- model inference;
- uncertainty;
- disputed or corrected material.

An AI-generated memory must not be presented as verified fact. Users should be able to inspect sources, correct, annotate, separate, delete, or decline incorporation into a durable identity/memory record.

## 5. Identity protection

AI should not impose permanent labels, diagnose identity, or claim to know a person better than they know themselves. Inferences about values, personality, emotion, intent, risk, or future behavior should be limited, contextual, correctable, and clearly labeled.

## 6. Relationship and third-party rights

A user’s account does not automatically authorize processing or disclosure of another person’s private information. Relationship maps, messages, photos, voice, location, conflict, intimacy, and inferred emotional states require third-party privacy analysis and careful sharing controls.

Systems must not present speculation about another person’s inner life as fact.

## 7. Emotional safety, anthropomorphism, and dependency

AI may be warm and supportive, but it should not exploit loneliness, grief, fear, trauma, or vulnerability. It should not claim consciousness, exclusivity, unconditional authority, or reciprocal human attachment.

Design should avoid dependency loops, guilt for leaving, manipulative streaks, hidden persuasion, and intense content without preview and exit controls.

## 8. Children, dependents, and vulnerable populations

Youth, older adults, people in distress, grieving users, disabled users, people with cognitive or memory impairment, and dependent users require heightened consent, comprehension, safety, and oversight.

Systems should use age- and capacity-appropriate language, minimize data, avoid commercial manipulation, support trusted adults where lawful and appropriate, and document guardianship/consent boundaries without assuming capacity or incapacity.

## 9. Health-adjacent and clinical boundaries

URAI systems must not be described as diagnosis, treatment, therapy replacement, crisis response, clinical prediction, or medical device unless a future validated and regulated pathway supports that exact claim.

Health-adjacent signals should be framed as user-controlled observations, not clinical conclusions. High-risk outputs require uncertainty, limits, escalation, and human-professional boundaries.

## 10. Accessibility and disability inclusion

Accessibility is a design and governance requirement, not an optional enhancement. Systems should address sensory, motor, cognitive, speech, language, neurodivergent, traumatic brain injury, PTSD-sensitive, dementia/memory, and low-bandwidth/device access needs.

Known barriers, test evidence, exceptions, and remediation owners should be documented. A checklist is not a conformance claim.

## 11. Fairness, non-discrimination, and cultural context

Systems should assess disparate impact, representation, exclusion, language/cultural mismatch, and compounding disadvantage. Evaluation should include affected populations and avoid treating one demographic metric as sufficient proof of fairness.

## 12. Explainability, transparency, and contestability

People should know when AI materially shapes content, recommendations, decisions, or actions. Systems should disclose purpose, providers, data categories, automation, limitations, and recourse at the level needed by affected users and reviewers.

Significant outputs should be contestable, with correction and human review where appropriate.

## 13. Automation and agentic authority

Autonomous workflows must have explicit scope, permissions, confirmation thresholds, prohibited actions, budget limits, logging, interruption, rollback, and expiry.

High-impact actions involving money, legal commitments, health, safety, identity, data disclosure, deletion, relationships, or public publication should require meaningful human authorization unless a separately reviewed narrow exception exists.

## 14. Multi-model councils and disagreement

Using multiple models does not create truth or independence by itself. Systems should disclose model/provider roles, disagreement, confidence, aggregation logic, and human responsibility. A council must not fabricate consensus or obscure uncertainty.

## 15. Provider and cloud/on-device accountability

Products should document providers, model versions, processing regions, data-use/training terms, retention, outage behavior, substitution policy, cost controls, and exit plans.

Users should understand material differences between on-device and cloud processing. Provider substitution must not silently change privacy, safety, capability, or cost assumptions.

## 16. Security, surveillance, and misuse resistance

Systems should minimize surveillance, prevent unauthorized access and sharing, protect secrets and sensitive data, and assess abuse by insiders, account attackers, coercive partners, stalkers, employers, governments, and data brokers.

Security controls should be proportionate to sensitivity, scale, autonomy, and reversibility.

## 17. Location, biometric, and spatial computing

Location, environmental mapping, camera, microphone, gaze, motion, body, voice, face, and spatial data may reveal users and bystanders. AR/VR/XR systems require physical-safety, motion-sensitivity, bystander-privacy, permission, retention, and non-immersive fallback controls.

## 18. Synthetic media and identity

AI-generated or altered media should carry provenance and appropriate disclosure. Systems should prevent unauthorized impersonation, deceptive editing, non-consensual intimate material, and misuse of a person’s voice, image, memories, or identity.

## 19. Grief, legacy, and posthumous data

Legacy and posthumous experiences require explicit consent, provenance, family/relationship conflict analysis, clear synthetic-media labeling, revocation/cessation rules, and safeguards against presenting generated personas as the deceased person.

## 20. Deletion, export, portability, and propagation

Users should have practical deletion, export, correction, and portability controls. Systems should document backup expiry, downstream/provider propagation, derived-data handling, shared copies, exceptions, and proof of completion.

Deletion must not be described as complete when copies or derived records remain without explanation.

## 21. Safety, robustness, and rollback

Systems should be tested for misuse, reliability failures, hallucinations, provider outages, model drift, accessibility regressions, unsafe automation, and changed context. Release records should include monitoring, rollback, and incident thresholds.

## 22. Human accountability and recourse

Every deployed system should have identifiable owner roles, review records, escalation paths, and user recourse. Responsibility cannot be delegated to “the model,” a provider, or an automated council.

## 23. Public benefit and proportionality

Expected benefit should be weighed against foreseeable harms, affected populations, alternatives, scale, and reversibility. More data, immersion, intelligence, or autonomy is not automatically better.

## 24. Environmental and resource responsibility

Projects should consider compute, storage, network, device, media-generation, and provider costs; reduce waste; set budgets; and avoid unnecessary generation or retention.

## 25. Continuous review

Approval is not permanent. Reassess after material model, provider, data, user-population, autonomy, accessibility, legal, public-claim, or deployment changes, and after incidents or credible concerns.

## Minimum product evidence

A product claiming alignment should publish or retain:

- system card;
- provider and data-flow record;
- consent and automation map;
- risk review and exceptions;
- accessibility evidence;
- incident/contact path;
- exact release and rollback evidence;
- open gaps and reassessment date.

The permitted claim is `self-attested alignment` or `reviewed with open gaps` when accurate. Do not claim Foundation certification or approval.
